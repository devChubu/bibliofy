Buck Converter Fundamentals

A comprehensive guide to step-down DC-DC conversion — from first-principles switching theory through practical component selection. Designed for power electronics engineers who want to understand how buck converters really work.

Chapter 1 The Buck Topology

~10 min read
Key question: How does a buck converter use high-frequency switching to produce a lower, regulated DC output from a higher DC input?
Prerequisites

This guide assumes familiarity with basic circuit theory (Ohm’s law, Kirchhoff’s laws), inductor and capacitor fundamentals (V = L · di/dt, I = C · dv/dt), and basic semiconductor device behavior (MOSFETs as switches, diode forward drop). No prior power electronics experience is required.

Why Step-Down Conversion

Many electronic systems receive power from a source whose voltage is higher than what the load requires. A 12V automotive battery must supply a 3.3V microcontroller. A 48V telecom bus must feed 1.0V processor cores. A USB 5V supply must power a 1.8V sensor. In every case, the voltage must be reduced — and it must be done efficiently.

The simplest approach is a linear regulator, which acts as a variable resistor in series with the load. The regulator drops excess voltage as heat: Ploss = (Vin − Vout) × Iout. For a 12V-to-3.3V conversion at 1A, that’s 8.7W dissipated as heat — a 27.5% efficiency. At higher currents or larger voltage drops, the heat becomes impractical.

Key Concept: Switching vs. Linear Regulation

A switching regulator (like the buck converter) achieves high efficiency by rapidly alternating between two states: fully on (switch closed, near-zero voltage drop) and fully off (switch open, zero current). Since power loss is V × I, and one of these terms is always near zero, the theoretical loss is minimal. Practical efficiencies of 85–97% are routine.

The buck converter is the most fundamental and widely used switching topology. It steps voltage down (hence "buck" — as in reducing). Its complement, the boost converter, steps voltage up. Together with the buck-boost and other derived topologies, they form the foundation of modern power conversion.

Basic Switching Operation

A buck converter has four essential components: a high-side switch (typically an N-channel or P-channel MOSFET), a low-side switch or freewheeling diode, an inductor (L), and an output capacitor (Cout). The circuit alternates between two states at a fixed switching frequency fSW (typically 100 kHz to several MHz).

Figure 1.1 — Synchronous buck converter schematic. Q1 (high-side) and Q2 (low-side) alternate switching. L and COUT form the output LC filter.

State 1 — ON phase (Q1 closed, Q2 open): Current flows from Vin through Q1, through the inductor L, into Cout and the load. The voltage across the inductor is VL = Vin − Vout (positive), so inductor current ramps up linearly: ΔI = (Vin − Vout) × ton / L.

State 2 — OFF phase (Q1 open, Q2 closed): Q1 turns off. The inductor current cannot change instantaneously, so it forces current to flow through Q2 (or the freewheeling diode) to maintain the current path. Now VL = −Vout (negative), and inductor current ramps down: ΔI = Vout × toff / L.

Dead-Time Is Critical

In a synchronous buck (using two MOSFETs), Q1 and Q2 must never be on simultaneously. This would create a short from Vin to ground — a “shoot-through” event that destroys the FETs and wastes enormous energy. All buck converter controllers insert a small “dead time” (typically 10–50 ns) between turning one FET off and the other on. During dead time, the body diode of Q2 conducts.

Duty Cycle and the Fundamental Equation

In steady state, the net volt-seconds across the inductor over one switching period must be zero (volt-second balance). During ton, the inductor sees (Vin − Vout). During toff, it sees (−Vout). Setting the total volt-seconds to zero:

(Vin − Vout) × ton = Vout × toff

Since D = ton / T and (1 − D) = toff / T, solving for Vout gives the fundamental buck converter equation:

Vout = D × Vin

where D is the duty cycle (0 ≤ D ≤ 1). For a 12V input producing 3.3V output, D = 3.3 / 12 = 0.275 (27.5%). The controller’s PWM signal spends 27.5% of each switching cycle with Q1 on.

python
def buck_duty_cycle(v_in, v_out, v_diode=0.0):
    """Calculate buck converter duty cycle.

    Args:
        v_in:    Input voltage (V)
        v_out:   Desired output voltage (V)
        v_diode: Diode forward drop (V), 0 for synchronous buck

    Returns:
        Duty cycle (0 to 1)
    """
    if v_out >= v_in:
        raise ValueError("Buck converter requires V_out < V_in")
    # For asynchronous buck with diode: D = (V_out + V_diode) / (V_in + V_diode)
    d = (v_out + v_diode) / (v_in + v_diode)
    return d

# Example: 12V to 3.3V synchronous buck
d = buck_duty_cycle(12.0, 3.3)
print(f"Duty cycle: {d:.3f} ({d*100:.1f}%)")
# Output: Duty cycle: 0.275 (27.5%)

# Example: 12V to 5V with Schottky diode (0.4V drop)
d = buck_duty_cycle(12.0, 5.0, v_diode=0.4)
print(f"Duty cycle with diode: {d:.3f} ({d*100:.1f}%)")
# Output: Duty cycle with diode: 0.435 (43.5%)
The diode forward voltage slightly increases the required duty cycle in asynchronous designs.
Practical Duty Cycle Limits

Real controllers cannot achieve D = 0% or D = 100%. Most controllers have a minimum on-time (ton,min) of 50–150 ns and a maximum duty cycle of 85–100%. At very low D (high step-down ratio), consider using a two-stage approach or a controller designed for low duty cycle operation. At the high end, look for “100% duty cycle” or “low-dropout” buck converters that can pass through when Vin is close to Vout.

Key Takeaway

The buck converter achieves efficient voltage step-down by chopping the input with a high-frequency switch and filtering the result with an LC network. The output voltage is set by the duty cycle: Vout = D × Vin. This elegant relationship comes directly from volt-second balance on the inductor — the same principle underpins all inductor-based converters.

Chapter 2 CCM vs DCM Operation

~12 min read
Key question: What determines whether inductor current flows continuously or drops to zero each switching cycle, and how does this affect converter behavior?

Continuous Conduction Mode (CCM)

In Continuous Conduction Mode, the inductor current never reaches zero during a switching cycle. Current always flows through the inductor — it ramps up during the ON phase and ramps down during the OFF phase, but the minimum value (the valley) stays above zero.

CCM is the “normal” operating mode for most buck converters. When you use the equation Vout = D × Vin, you are implicitly assuming CCM operation. In CCM, the output voltage depends only on the duty cycle and input voltage — it is independent of load current. This makes the control loop straightforward.

Key Concept: Inductor Current Waveform in CCM

In CCM, the inductor current is a triangle wave superimposed on a DC offset (the load current). The peak-to-peak ripple current ΔIL is determined by the inductance, switching frequency, and voltage across the inductor during each state. The DC component equals the output load current: IL,avg = Iout.

Figure 2.1 — Inductor current waveform in Continuous Conduction Mode. The valley current remains above zero.

The peak-to-peak inductor current ripple in CCM is:

ΔIL = (Vin − Vout) × D / (fSW × L)

This can also be written as ΔIL = Vout × (1 − D) / (fSW × L), since the volt-seconds during ON and OFF phases are equal.

python
def inductor_ripple_current(v_in, v_out, f_sw, l_h):
    """Calculate peak-to-peak inductor ripple current in CCM.

    Args:
        v_in:  Input voltage (V)
        v_out: Output voltage (V)
        f_sw:  Switching frequency (Hz)
        l_h:   Inductance (Henries)

    Returns:
        Peak-to-peak ripple current (A)
    """
    d = v_out / v_in
    delta_il = (v_in - v_out) * d / (f_sw * l_h)
    return delta_il

# Example: 12V to 3.3V, 500 kHz, 4.7 uH inductor
delta_i = inductor_ripple_current(12.0, 3.3, 500e3, 4.7e-6)
print(f"Ripple current: {delta_i:.3f} A ({delta_i*1000:.0f} mA)")
# Output: Ripple current: 1.356 A (1356 mA)

Discontinuous Conduction Mode (DCM)

In Discontinuous Conduction Mode, the inductor current reaches zero during the OFF phase and stays at zero for the remainder of the switching cycle. This introduces a third state: the “dead time” or “idle” period where no energy is being transferred. DCM occurs at light loads, when the ripple current amplitude exceeds twice the average load current.

In DCM, the simple Vout = D × Vin relationship no longer holds. The output voltage becomes load-dependent:

Vout = Vin × D2 / (D2 + D22 × K)

where K = 2L / (Rload × T) is a dimensionless conduction parameter. This load dependence complicates the control loop and can degrade transient response.

CCM/DCM Transition Challenges

Some controllers are designed to operate only in CCM. If the load drops enough for the converter to enter DCM, the control loop — designed for CCM’s single-pole/single-zero transfer function — may become unstable. Synchronous buck converters can force CCM operation even at light loads by allowing negative inductor current (current flowing backward from output to input through Q2). This wastes some energy at light load but maintains predictable control dynamics.

Figure 2.2 — Inductor current waveform in Discontinuous Conduction Mode. Current reaches zero and stays there during the dead time.

Boundary Condition and Ripple Calculations

The boundary between CCM and DCM occurs when the valley of the inductor current exactly touches zero. At this point, the average inductor current equals half the ripple: Iout,boundary = ΔIL / 2. Below this load current, the converter enters DCM.

python
def ccm_dcm_boundary(v_in, v_out, f_sw, l_h):
    """Calculate the CCM/DCM boundary load current.

    Below this current, the converter enters DCM.

    Args:
        v_in:  Input voltage (V)
        v_out: Output voltage (V)
        f_sw:  Switching frequency (Hz)
        l_h:   Inductance (Henries)

    Returns:
        Boundary current (A) - converter enters DCM below this
    """
    d = v_out / v_in
    delta_il = (v_in - v_out) * d / (f_sw * l_h)
    i_boundary = delta_il / 2.0
    return i_boundary

def output_voltage_ripple(delta_il, f_sw, c_out, esr=0.0):
    """Calculate output voltage ripple (peak-to-peak).

    Two components: capacitive (charge-based) and ESR-based.

    Args:
        delta_il: Inductor ripple current, peak-to-peak (A)
        f_sw:     Switching frequency (Hz)
        c_out:    Output capacitance (F)
        esr:      Capacitor ESR (ohms)

    Returns:
        Tuple of (total_ripple_v, capacitive_ripple_v, esr_ripple_v)
    """
    # Capacitive ripple: delta_V_cap = delta_I_L / (8 * f_sw * C_out)
    v_cap = delta_il / (8.0 * f_sw * c_out)
    # ESR ripple: delta_V_esr = delta_I_L * ESR
    v_esr = delta_il * esr
    # Total (RSS for uncorrelated, but often just summed conservatively)
    v_total = v_cap + v_esr
    return v_total, v_cap, v_esr

# Example: 12V to 3.3V, 500 kHz, 4.7 uH
i_bnd = ccm_dcm_boundary(12.0, 3.3, 500e3, 4.7e-6)
print(f"CCM/DCM boundary: {i_bnd*1000:.0f} mA")
# Output: CCM/DCM boundary: 678 mA

# Output ripple: 1.356A ripple, 500kHz, 22uF MLCC (ESR ~ 3 mohm)
v_tot, v_cap, v_esr = output_voltage_ripple(1.356, 500e3, 22e-6, 0.003)
print(f"Capacitive ripple: {v_cap*1000:.1f} mV")
print(f"ESR ripple:        {v_esr*1000:.1f} mV")
print(f"Total ripple:      {v_tot*1000:.1f} mV")
# Output:
# Capacitive ripple: 15.4 mV
# ESR ripple:        4.1 mV
# Total ripple:      19.5 mV

Deep Dive: Forced-CCM and Pulse-Skipping Modes

Modern buck controllers often offer multiple light-load modes:

  • Forced CCM (FCCM): Q2 stays active even when inductor current reverses. Maintains fixed frequency and predictable noise spectrum, but wastes power at light load due to circulating currents. Preferred when EMI is the primary concern.
  • Diode Emulation / DCM: Q2 turns off when inductor current reaches zero, preventing reverse current. Better light-load efficiency but variable switching frequency.
  • Pulse-Skipping: The controller skips entire switching pulses when the output is above regulation. The converter bursts a few pulses, then goes idle. Very efficient at light loads but creates low-frequency noise components that are hard to filter.
  • PFM (Pulse-Frequency Modulation): Fixed on-time pulses at a variable repetition rate. Provides a smooth transition between heavy and light loads with good efficiency across the range.

The choice between these modes depends on the application’s sensitivity to output ripple, EMI requirements, and the range of load currents encountered.

Key Takeaway

CCM and DCM are not “good” and “bad” — they are different operating regimes. CCM gives predictable voltage conversion and simpler control. DCM can offer better light-load efficiency. The boundary between them is set by the inductor value, switching frequency, and load current. Design for CCM at your maximum load, then decide how you want the converter to behave at light load.

Inductor Sizing

The inductor is the most critical passive component in a buck converter. It stores energy during the ON phase and delivers it to the load during the OFF phase. The choice of inductance value involves a fundamental trade-off: a larger inductor reduces current ripple (less stress on capacitors, lower EMI) but is physically bigger, more expensive, and responds more slowly to load transients.

A widely used rule of thumb sets the peak-to-peak inductor ripple current to 20–40% of the maximum DC load current. This balances ripple, efficiency, transient response, and physical size.

def size_inductor(v_in, v_out, i_out_max, f_sw, ripple_ratio=0.3):
    """Calculate required inductance for a buck converter.

    Args:
        v_in:         Input voltage (V)
        v_out:        Output voltage (V)
        i_out_max:    Maximum output current (A)
        f_sw:         Switching frequency (Hz)
        ripple_ratio: Desired delta_I_L / I_out (0.2 to 0.4 typical)

    Returns:
        dict with inductance (H), ripple current (A), peak current (A),
              and CCM/DCM boundary current (A)
    """
    d = v_out / v_in
    delta_il = ripple_ratio * i_out_max  # Target ripple current

    # L = (V_in - V_out) * D / (f_sw * delta_I_L)
    l_h = (v_in - v_out) * d / (f_sw * delta_il)

    i_peak = i_out_max + delta_il / 2.0
    i_valley = i_out_max - delta_il / 2.0
    i_boundary = delta_il / 2.0

    return {
        "inductance_uH": l_h * 1e6,
        "ripple_current_A": delta_il,
        "peak_current_A": i_peak,
        "valley_current_A": i_valley,
        "ccm_boundary_A": i_boundary,
    }

# Design example: 12V to 3.3V at 3A, 500 kHz, 30% ripple
result = size_inductor(12.0, 3.3, 3.0, 500e3, 0.3)
for key, val in result.items():
    print(f"  {key}: {val:.3f}")
# Output:
#   inductance_uH: 5.339
#   ripple_current_A: 0.900
#   peak_current_A: 3.450
#   valley_current_A: 2.550
#   ccm_boundary_A: 0.450

# Select nearest standard value: 4.7 uH or 5.6 uH

Inductor Selection Checklist

When choosing an inductor, verify these four specifications against your design requirements:

  • Inductance (L): The calculated value ± tolerance at DC bias. Inductance drops under DC bias — check the L vs. I curve in the datasheet. A ferrite inductor rated 4.7 µH at 0A may be only 3.5 µH at your full load current.
  • Saturation current (Isat): Must exceed Ipeak with margin. At saturation, inductance collapses and current surges — potentially destroying the MOSFET.
  • RMS current rating (Irms): Must exceed the RMS inductor current (approximately equal to Iout in CCM) without excessive temperature rise.
  • DCR (DC resistance): Lower is better for efficiency. Ploss = Irms² × DCR. A typical 4.7 µH / 3A inductor has DCR around 30–80 mΩ.

Capacitor Selection

Both input and output capacitors are essential. The output capacitor (Cout) filters the inductor’s triangle-wave ripple current and supplies charge during load transients. The input capacitor (Cin) supplies the pulsed current demanded by the high-side switch and reduces voltage stress on Vin.

Output Capacitor

The output voltage ripple has two components: the capacitive term (ΔQ stored and released by Cout) and the ESR term (ripple current flowing through the capacitor’s equivalent series resistance):

ΔVout = ΔIL / (8 × fSW × Cout) + ΔIL × ESR

For ceramic capacitors (MLCCs), ESR is typically 1–10 mΩ, and the capacitive term dominates. For electrolytic capacitors, ESR is much higher (10–200 mΩ), and the ESR term dominates.

Ceramic Capacitor DC Bias Derating

Class II ceramic capacitors (X5R, X7R) lose significant capacitance under DC bias. A 22 µF / 6.3V capacitor in an 0805 package may retain only 10–12 µF at 3.3V DC bias — a 45–55% loss. Always check the manufacturer’s DC bias curves. Use higher voltage ratings (e.g., 10V or 16V for a 3.3V output) or larger package sizes to mitigate derating. TDK’s SEAT tool and Murata’s SimSurfing provide accurate DC bias data.

Input Capacitor

The input capacitor sees a pulsed current waveform: during the ON phase, it must supply the full load current minus whatever the source can deliver. The RMS current through Cin is:

ICin,rms = Iout × √(D × (1 − D))

This current is maximized at D = 0.5 (50% duty cycle) where ICin,rms = 0.5 × Iout. The input capacitor must handle this RMS current without excessive heating. Multiple ceramic capacitors in parallel are common.

Deep Dive: Load Transient Response

The output capacitor’s most demanding job is not filtering steady-state ripple — it is handling load transients. When the load current steps from light to heavy (e.g., a processor waking from sleep), the capacitor must supply the difference until the control loop adjusts the duty cycle.

The output voltage undershoot during a load step ΔIload has two components:

  • ESR drop: ΔVESR = ΔIload × ESR (instantaneous)
  • Capacitive droop: ΔVcap = ΔIload × tresponse / Cout (depends on control loop bandwidth)

The total undershoot must stay within the load’s voltage tolerance. For a typical processor requiring ±3% tolerance on a 1.0V rail, the total undershoot must be less than 30 mV. This often drives the capacitor selection more than steady-state ripple does.

Rule of thumb: Cout ≥ ΔIload / (2π × fcrossover × ΔVallowed), where fcrossover is the control loop bandwidth.

MOSFET losses have two main components:

  • Conduction loss: Pcond = Irms² × RDS(on). Lower RDS(on) is better, but it comes with larger die size, more gate charge, and higher cost.
  • Switching loss: Psw = ½ × Vin × Iout × (trise + tfall) × fSW. Faster switching reduces losses but increases dV/dt and di/dt, worsening EMI. Gate charge (Qg) determines how fast the MOSFET can switch.

The high-side and low-side FETs have different optimization criteria:

def mosfet_losses(v_in, i_out, rds_on, qg_nc, f_sw, v_driver=5.0,
                   t_rise_ns=10, t_fall_ns=10, duty=None, v_out=None):
    """Estimate MOSFET power losses in a buck converter.

    Args:
        v_in:       Input voltage (V)
        i_out:      Output current (A)
        rds_on:     On-resistance (ohms)
        qg_nc:      Total gate charge (nC)
        f_sw:       Switching frequency (Hz)
        v_driver:   Gate driver voltage (V)
        t_rise_ns:  Rise time (ns)
        t_fall_ns:  Fall time (ns)
        duty:       Duty cycle (if None, calculated from v_out)
        v_out:      Output voltage (V), used if duty is None

    Returns:
        dict of loss components (W)
    """
    if duty is None:
        duty = v_out / v_in

    # Conduction loss (high-side sees duty D, low-side sees 1-D)
    p_cond_hs = i_out**2 * rds_on * duty
    p_cond_ls = i_out**2 * rds_on * (1 - duty)

    # Switching loss (primarily high-side)
    t_sw = (t_rise_ns + t_fall_ns) * 1e-9
    p_sw = 0.5 * v_in * i_out * t_sw * f_sw

    # Gate drive loss
    qg_c = qg_nc * 1e-9
    p_gate = qg_c * v_driver * f_sw

    return {
        "P_cond_HS_W": round(p_cond_hs, 4),
        "P_cond_LS_W": round(p_cond_ls, 4),
        "P_switching_W": round(p_sw, 4),
        "P_gate_W": round(p_gate, 4),
        "P_total_W": round(p_cond_hs + p_cond_ls + p_sw + p_gate, 4),
    }

# Example: 12V to 3.3V at 3A, 500 kHz
# Using a FET with Rds(on)=25 mohm, Qg=15 nC
losses = mosfet_losses(
    v_in=12.0, i_out=3.0, rds_on=0.025,
    qg_nc=15, f_sw=500e3, v_out=3.3,
    t_rise_ns=8, t_fall_ns=12
)
for key, val in losses.items():
    print(f"  {key}: {val:.4f}")

MOSFET loss estimation. Gate drive and switching losses scale with frequency; conduction losses scale with I². The figure of merit (FOM = Qg × RDS(on)) captures the fundamental conduction-vs-switching trade-off.
Key Concept: Figure of Merit (FOM)

The product Qg × RDS(on) is the MOSFET’s figure of merit for switching applications. A lower FOM indicates a better device. Reducing RDS(on) requires a larger die, which increases gate area and Qg. The FOM captures this fundamental trade-off: you cannot minimize both simultaneously. Modern power MOSFETs achieve FOM values around 20–100 mΩ·nC for 30V-rated devices.

Key Takeaway

Component selection is where theory meets practice. Size the inductor for 20–40% ripple ratio, verify saturation current with margin. Select output capacitors based on ripple and transient requirements, accounting for DC bias derating on ceramics. Choose MOSFETs by their figure of merit, and remember that the high-side and low-side FETs optimize for different loss mechanisms. When in doubt, start with the reference design in the controller’s datasheet — it represents the manufacturer’s tested starting point.
